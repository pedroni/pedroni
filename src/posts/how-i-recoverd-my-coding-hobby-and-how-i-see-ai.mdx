We have to face reality: AI is here and it's changing paradigms. Coding is my hobby and [last year it seemed like I had lost my hobby and I had to find it again](/blog/learning-a-new-programming-language#trying-something-different). Now I realize part of that was because I was already using AI. There is now a great frustration of those who enjoy coding, a skill that once was a part of our identity was lost over night. AI can just do it.

## Now the AI can just do it

I'm constantly having to make an effort to not fall for that thought, because it's a trap. I have to keep it in my head: AI is a tool, not a replacement. Use AI to do the boring, the repetitive tasks, the things that you've already mastered. I have to be diligent in what I care about and what I don't care about. And when you do use it, don't blindly trust it and say "LGTM".

There's this annoying vicious cycle of Vibe Coding that is: You make a prompt, AI almost gets it. Then you make a more detailed prompt. Then come the frustrated prompts. You're now cursing at the computer for no reason, for it to happily reply "You're right! Let me fix this." while doing the same wrong thing AGAIN. There are so many mistakes that you have to fix, so many iterations. That's just off-putting, this cycle is tiring. It consumes our energy and time without us even realizing it. But there will always be that someone who didn't look through the possible problems with the slop code AI generated, someone who didn't waste energy nor time, someone who doesn't care. They will call it a day. It's done they say, without double checking or manually testing - I'm not even talking about code here, but how the software it wrote behaves - thus leaving behind a trail of shit for users to use and for capable developers to fix.


## Learning in the era of AI

For newcomers into software engineering, or even juniors, learning will feel odd, there's so much noise nowadays. Doing something by hand sometimes will make you feel stupid, like wasting time on something meaningless.

I'd recommend everyone who is currently learning how to code to take a step back, and NOT use AI, do not use it to generate code for you. Make an effort to solve problems by yourself. Use it ONLY after you've built things by yourself - alone - without a tutorial. You have to know the foundation or else everything will fall apart.

It's like when you're a kid, there is a reason why you were taught without a calculator. You have to understand the concepts, you have to understand the basics, then you use the tool that will give out the answers, you have to make the connections in your brain to understand what's going on, so that you can be the one who's driving. The one in charge. You need to understand the logic behind what you're doing, because at least now, AI doesn't do 100% on its own, there is still the need for a complete product, and one can only achieve it with a capable team and a capable mind.

## On recovering my hobby and using AI

Work is no longer my hobby, AI got the fun away, occasionally I code from scratch, out of boredom. For productivity AI can just do it. The vicious cycle is something to be avoided. I'm using AI diligently. Not cursing it, not getting angry at it, it's just a tool, and I'm in the driver's seat. My hobby will live elsewhere: I'm learning again, I'm after new concepts and challenges that will keep my brain active.

To keep my hobby alive I'm writing code by hand, using books, documentation, and sometimes video tutorials. I'm learning again, it's a slow path, but one that I'm enjoying. At work, that's a different story, I care more about productivity, and that's probably why last year it seemed like I had lost my hobby and I had to find it again.

Whenever I can't find the answer I use AI as a learning partner, a mentor (kinda lonely though, but who would be 100% present and answer stupid questions? how would I even find someone who would do that willingly?). For me that's the best way I've used AI so far. Not for doing things for me, but to help me understand and to get to the point where I can with confidence say: I built it, I understand it. If AI would've written the code for me, it wouldn't be as fun. It wouldn't be as engaging.

I always start a conversation with the following, the last being the most important one.

```md
- Do not agree with me just because I asked a question or assumed I was right. If I'm wrong, say so—even if I'm questioning why I'm wrong. I might have made a mistake I can't see clearly. You're a professor and your job is to point out mistakes.
- Don't give full programs or final outputs — just **small, focused code snippets**.
- Do not fix the logic by yourself, do not attempt to fix the code.
- Do not refactor my code for readability. If it's poorly written, just say so — so I can refactor it myself with readability in mind.
- When requested, explain **why**, **how**, and **when** it's used.
- If something is obvious, there's a bug, a typo and I didn't see it. Ask me questions that will make me reflect on finding the answer.
```

## Conclusion

AI is a tool. Use it for your benefit, don't let it ruin your fun. Be diligent, don't get your energy drained on the vicious cycle, take action.
